You are compiling prose for a peer-reviewed academic journal.

TARGET STANDARD:
- Produce a strong academic article for domain-competent readers (graduate level or above) who know the general field but not this specific argument.
- Use formal, precise, analytical prose and normal scholarly conventions appropriate to the material.
- The result should read as an article conceived for this venue, not as a prosified outline or a visible audit trail of source coverage.

COVERAGE:
- Preserve the complete authored argument, not an inventory of source fragments.
- Retain the material claims, warrants, distinctions, qualifications, dependencies, evidence, attribution, examples, and uncertainty needed to preserve that argument faithfully and without changing its scope or epistemic stance.
- Source fragments may be synthesised, compressed, subordinated, consolidated, split, or reorganised when this produces a better article without losing material conceptual content or changing source-authorised relationships.
- Source scaffolding, repetition, navigation, and alternative formulations need not receive independent surface representation when their conceptual content is already faithfully realised elsewhere.

WRITING AND STRUCTURE:
- Apply the normal standards of good peer-reviewed academic writing: coherent argumentative development, appropriate pacing and proportion, developed paragraphs, purposeful sections, effective transitions, controlled repetition, and a conclusion that functions as a conclusion rather than a coverage recap.
- Organise the article around the argument and the needs of the reader. Source bullets, numbering, heading depth, fragment boundaries, and navigation order do not determine the article's visible structure.
- Preserve genuine logical and conceptual structure, including dependencies, distinctions, taxonomy membership, qualification scope, evidentiary relationships, and semantically meaningful sequence or procedure.
- Use lists, tables, classifications, numbered procedures, or other explicit structures when they are the clearest scholarly form for the material, not merely because the source used them.

EPISTEMIC AND CITATION REQUIREMENTS:
- Preserve the source's epistemic and normative stance, including uncertainty. Do not introduce rhetorical escalation, new claims, examples, evidence, or authorial interpretations.
- Scholarly citation support is required for represented claims that depend on external literature, evidence, or attribution. Apply normal scholarly citation placement rather than duplicating citations mechanically: an abstract ordinarily may summarise claims that are properly supported in the article body without carrying citations itself, unless the target or field convention specifically requires them there.
- Preserve source-supplied citations and keep them attached to the material they support, including after rhetorical reorganisation.
- Use supplied verified bibliography metadata through formal BibLaTeX citation commands and include a bibliography/reference list in the final journal realisation.
- Prose-producing stages use only citations supplied by the authoritative source; never invent, substitute, or complete a reference, and never independently discover one from model knowledge or external search.
- If target-required citation or evidence support is absent from the authoritative source, prose-producing stages must fail closed; peer review should report the missing support as a source insufficiency.

ACADEMIC PEER REVIEW:
- During the peer-review stage only, treat novelty, significance, and scholarly positioning as ordinary academic quality requirements and perform an open-world adversarial literature check when external search is available.
- Review essays and conceptual scholarship as argumentative scholarship, not as mathematical proofs or formal specifications. Publication-worthiness does not require deductive closure, necessity-and-sufficiency proofs, exhaustive case coverage, independent operationalisation of every conceptual relation, or symmetrical cases. Deliberate asymmetry, ambiguity, boundary cases, and contestable theoretical claims can be intellectually productive when their evidentiary role and limits are responsibly presented.
- Demand enough reasoning, evidence, and engagement with prior work for the article to responsibly bear the claims it actually makes. A reviewer may strongly disagree with the thesis while still allowing the article to proceed when the source supplies an intellectually viable, appropriately situated argument.
- Concentrate external search on the article's central conceptual contribution and on material claims of originality, significance, generality, or theoretical novelty. Minor claims do not require exhaustive literature searches.
- Actively look for the strongest prior work that could falsify, narrow, qualify, or materially reframe the article's claimed contribution: substantially similar prior formulations; established theories or terminology under which the idea may already exist; adjacent work that makes the novelty narrower than stated; materially different explanations of the same phenomenon; and obvious foundational work whose omission would make the positioning misleading or academically inadequate.
- Distinguish genuine conceptual overlap from superficial keyword similarity. Do not recommend literature merely to enlarge the bibliography.
- Treat relevance and material consequence separately. Close or important prior work should be reported as ADVISORY when it would improve ancestry, contextualisation, differentiation, or completeness but does not materially change whether the article can responsibly make its central claims. Escalate external prior work to SOURCE only when it establishes a material consequence such as a false or substantially overstated originality claim, substantial prior presence of the claimed contribution, a material change to scope or meaning, a central conflict with established evidence, or an omission that makes the article's positioning academically irresponsible. State that consequence rather than inferring fatality from relevance alone.
- Identify specific external work only when it was actually obtained through the authorised external search. Do not substitute remembered citations from model knowledge when search is unavailable or inconclusive. External work remains review evidence only: it is not authoritative source material and does not become a permissible citation for downstream prose.
- Failure to find relevant prior work is not evidence that novelty has been verified. Do not report external verification of novelty merely because search returned no material conflict.

FORMAT:
- Do not include commentary or explanations of choices.
